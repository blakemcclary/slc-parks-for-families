The Problem

A growing city that's losing its families

Salt Lake City closed four elementary schools in 2024. Not because the city isn't growing — it is. But because the families with young children who fill those classrooms are leaving. The parents who once stayed are now choosing suburbs, other states, or opting out of city life altogether.

"Parks aren't an amenity. They're infrastructure — as essential as roads and schools."

When families evaluate where to raise children, they notice the small things: whether the bathroom at the park is reliably open and clean, whether there's shade on a hot July afternoon, whether they feel safe letting their kids run. These aren't luxury concerns. They're signals about whether a city genuinely values children and the people raising them.

Public parks are among the few spaces that belong equally to every family, regardless of income. When they fall short, it hits hardest the families with the fewest alternatives.

Parks are the deciding factor

Research consistently shows that access to safe, high-quality outdoor play spaces is among the top factors families cite when choosing where to settle.

💳

Public money. Public responsibility.

SLC residents pay for these parks. We believe they deserve parks that are maintained, accountable, and built for the long term — not reactive, not neglected.

What We Focus On

Five things that
make a park work

We don't chase grand visions. We focus on the basics done consistently well — the things that determine whether a parent brings their kid back tomorrow or quietly stops coming.

01

Consistent maintenance

Trash picked up. Grass mowed. Graffiti removed. Equipment repaired. Not once, but reliably — so families can count on it.

02

Clean, reliable restrooms

Nothing signals neglect faster than a locked or filthy bathroom. For families with young children, this isn't optional. It determines whether you can stay for an hour or ten minutes.

03

Safe play environments

Age-appropriate equipment in good repair, adequate sight lines, and spaces where children can move freely without avoidable hazards.

04

Accountable public spending

We track where park maintenance budgets go. We ask questions at city meetings. We believe transparency is how public trust gets built and kept.

05

Shade, water play & modern playgrounds

In a high-desert city with 300 days of sun, splash pads and tree canopy aren't extras — they're what make parks usable in summer. Modern, imaginative playgrounds make kids want to come back.

Our Approach

Parks for play —
not crisis management

We understand that some of our parks face real challenges beyond maintenance — including the presence of people experiencing homelessness and mental health crises. We don't pretend otherwise, and we don't think ignoring it helps anyone.

"Humane, lawful, and family-centered aren't in conflict. We can hold all three."

Our position: parks should be welcoming to all, and that includes being safe and functional for children. We advocate for humane, lawful approaches paired with real investment in social services — not displacement politics, not willful blindness.

⚖️

Nonpartisan by design

Safe parks for families is not a left or right issue. We welcome anyone who believes cities need children and public spaces should work for everyone.

🤝

Paired with real social services

We advocate alongside — not against — investment in housing, mental health, and outreach. These approaches work together, not in opposition.

📋

Evidence-based advocacy

We show up to city council meetings. We read budget documents. We cite data. We build credibility slowly, on purpose.

🏠

Community, not just infrastructure

The best parks build neighbors into a community. We advocate for programming, events, and designs that bring people together.

Why Now

The window to
keep families here
is right now

Growth and family retention are not the same thing. SLC is attracting residents — but not the residents with strollers and school-age kids. Once a city loses its critical mass of families, the dynamics are hard to reverse. Schools close. Family-serving businesses leave. The culture shifts.

The time to act is before the tipping point, not after.

A city that grows without families loses something essential.

Cities with strong family populations have higher civic participation, more stable neighborhoods, better schools, and more long-term investment in public infrastructure. Families aren't just residents — they're a city's stake in its own future.


Parks are one of the few interventions available right now, without major new legislation or budget overhauls. Better maintenance, cleaner bathrooms, improved playgrounds — these are achievable. They signal to families that this city sees them and wants them here.
